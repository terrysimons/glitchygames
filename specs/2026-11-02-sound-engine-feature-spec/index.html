GlitchyGames Sound Engine — Feature Specification#

1. Vision#

Sound becomes a first-class retro feature of GlitchyGames, alongside the CRT power-on, the colour bars and the channel 3 screen [Owner]: something people notice and play with, not something that sits quietly underneath. The engine makes a simple beep effortless, a sophisticated instrument possible, and a sound that reacts continuously to gameplay natural to express.

It has the immediacy of a classic sound chip and the flexibility of a modern synthesizer. A developer picks a wave, a pitch, a length and an envelope in one line. A sound designer builds reusable instruments from named waves, blends, wavetables, envelopes, filters and modulation. A game connects those instruments to its own values (rpm, charge, health) without ever building an audio buffer.

Three promises shape everything else:

  1. Games stop "raw-dogging" audio [Owner]. Today each game assembles numpy arrays, mixer channels and frequency tables itself. The engine gives them a formal interface instead.
  2. One call gets a sound, and the first call compiles it [Owner]. audio.sound(...) returns a ready-to-play sound. The first request for a given sound compiles it (renders its audio, or prepares a live instrument). Every later request with the same arguments returns it from the engine's cache. Getting a sound never plays it; .play() does. Calling audio.sound(...) in setup is just the convention for paying the compile cost before gameplay. The code that loads a sound is the same code that uses it.
  3. The engine owns compiled sounds [Owner: "the engine tracks the temp files"]. The engine keeps the rendered audio and its cache. Games never handle buffers or files.

Example sounds the engine is designed for:

  • A coin pickup: two short pulse-wave notes.
  • A CRT switching on: a power click, the degauss thump, the flyback whine, static as the picture opens, then the colour bars' 1 kHz tone [Owner: "I would love the CRT power-on to have sounds"].
  • A vehicle: a pitched oscillator plus filtered noise. rpm continuously moves its pitch and brightness.
  • A lead instrument that slides across a wavetable of named waves (sine to ghost to square) as a game value rises.
  • A seeded procedural explosion that reproduces exactly in a bug report and renders to a WAV file without opening an audio device.

1.1 What exists today [Fact]#

The engine already has a solid base, and this spec builds on it [Owner: "we can absolutely build on existing infra"]:

Piece Where What it does
Synth toolkit src/glitchygames/audio/synth.py numpy waveforms (sine, square, sawtooth, triangle, noise; PolyBLEP on square and saw), frequency_curve, Envelope + apply_envelope, FIR low_pass/high_pass, distort, tremolo, mix, normalize, to_sound()
Recipes src/glitchygames/audio/recipes.py Tone, SoundRecipe, TOML [recipes.<name>] loading, SoundBank.play(name) / prerender()
Live voices src/glitchygames/audio/live.py LiveVoice: 100 ms chunks rendered from the game loop and queued on reserved mixer channels (--live-voice-pool, default 4)
Mixer pygame.mixer (pygame-ce 2.5.8, SDL 2.32.10) 44100 Hz, signed 16-bit, stereo, 512-frame buffer (CLAUDE.md, Sound Synthesis)
Replays src/glitchygames/replay/silence.py FrameSilencer stops sounds that silent rebuild/fast-forward frames start
Headless GameEngine (src/glitchygames/engine/game_engine.py) --headless restarts the mixer on SDL's dummy driver
Controller audio src/glitchygames/audio/controller_audio.py miniaudio capture and playback for a controller's own mic and speaker (DualSense)

How games use it today [Fact]:

  • Brave Adventurer (src/games/brave-adventurer/src/brave_adventurer_core/sounds.py) calls the synth functions directly and builds five pygame.mixer.Sounds at scene setup.
  • Mr. Muncher (src/games/mr-muncher/src/mr_muncher/sounds.py) and Clockwork Keep (src/games/clockwork-keep/src/clockwork_keep/sounds.py) write SoundRecipes with hand-typed NOTE_FREQUENCIES tables and prerender() a SoundBank at setup.
  • Clockwork Keep's elevator motor is a LiveVoice whose frequency follows the ride.
  • Paddle Slap loads WAV files through glitchygames.game_objects.sounds.load_sound.
  • The CRT power-on (src/glitchygames/crt_power_on.py) is silent. Its sound was already listed as a follow-up (plans/future-features.md #53).

All sound rendering today runs on the main (game-loop) thread with numpy [Fact]. Today's costs are small [Measured]:

  • Mr. Muncher's 11 recipes take 17.8 ms in total. The worst, film_snap, takes 8.5 ms.
  • Clockwork Keep's 7 recipes take 18.9 ms in total. The worst, victory, takes 9.7 ms.
  • A live voice's 100 ms chunk takes 0.15 ms.

Both games already pay the larger cost once, at setup. The compile-on-first-request model keeps compiles out of gameplay frames by convention, and §16.2 says how the gain is measured rather than assumed.

1.2 Vocabulary#

These terms are used the same way in both specs and in the public API.

Term Definition
Wave One cycle of a periodic shape, by name ('square', 'ghost') or built from points or harmonics
Wavetable An ordered row of waves with a continuous position that blends between neighbours
Source Something that produces a signal: an oscillator playing a wave or wavetable, a noise generator, or a sample player
Operator An oscillator plus envelope taking part in an FM arrangement
Instrument How something sounds: an immutable recipe of up to four sources, their envelopes, filters, modulation, effects and exposed parameters. It has no pitch of its own, like a piano nobody is touching
Sound What is played on an instrument: an instrument plus a pitch, notes or a chord, and a length. audio.sound(...) returns one, compiled and ready to play. A sound is either baked or live
Compile Turning a requested sound into something playable. For a baked sound that means rendering its audio. For a live sound it means validating it and building its wave tables. It happens on the first request, never again for the same arguments
Baked sound A sound whose audio was rendered once, at compile time, and is played from memory
Live sound A sound rendered while it plays, because exposed parameters change it continuously
Voice One playing instance of a sound, with its own state. A chord plays as one voice per note
Sequence Timed note events with a tempo and tracks (music; M5)
Bus A mixing destination with its own volume: music, effects, ui, ambience, dialogue
Output channel A speaker channel (left, right). Not a pygame mixer Channel
Mixer channel One of pygame's playback slots (pygame.mixer.Channel). Each playing voice uses one
Handle What .play() returns: a reference to one playing or pending voice
Profile A documented set of restrictions or defaults applied to authoring (Chip, Wavetable, FM, Sampler, Hybrid)

A four-source instrument playing one note is one voice. It costs four sources' worth of rendering, which a baked sound pays once, at compile time. Voices, sources and render cost are reported separately (§11).

2. Goals, non-goals and anti-goals#

Goals#

  1. A formal, small sound API every game can use instead of the mixer and numpy directly [Owner].
  2. One call, audio.sound(...), both loads and gets a sound; the first call compiles it, and calling it never plays it [Owner].
  3. Pitches written as notes or frequencies, and both styles feel first-class [Owner: "Allow both notes and frequency tables"].
  4. Named waves that can be mixed, blended, morphed in wavetables, drawn as points or built from harmonics, in code, in TOML files and later in an editor [Owner: "All of the above!"]. The numbers in those definitions get explanatory names [Owner].
  5. Sounds referred to by short name or by explicit path, both supported [Owner].
  6. Continuous game-driven sounds (vehicle, ambience, health) through named instrument parameters.
  7. The CRT power-on gets sound as the first thing built on the new interface [Owner].
  8. Demos and small games that show the sound system off [Owner: "build some sound demos - or games"].
  9. Reproducible, offline rendering to a file, with no audio device.
  10. Existing games keep working unchanged until they choose to migrate.

Non-goals (for this spec's milestones)#

  • Cycle-accurate or electrically accurate emulation of any real sound chip.
  • Transparent time-stretching, or pitch-shifting that keeps duration. Rate-based pitch changes also change duration (§4.4).
  • Sample-accurate response to arbitrary main-thread calls, or sync across machines.
  • An unrestricted modular node graph, or user code running inside rendering.
  • 3D/HRTF spatial audio.
  • A full instrument editor in the first milestone (§9.4 describes the later tool).

Anti-goals (would be failures even if they technically work)#

  • A second, competing audio device or music clock beside pygame.mixer that drifts out of sync.
  • An API where reading music is required. Frequencies and named steps are always offered.
  • Retro character that smears away a wave's intended sharp edges, or clicks and pops that come from bad engineering rather than design.
  • Gameplay hitches caused by sound work in a frame that the game did not ask for.
  • Copying recordings, ROM sound banks or proprietary instrument presets into the engine.
  • Breaking replays: a sound must never change game state, a random stream or a clock a replay serves.

3. Public workflows#

All examples use the API settled in the implementation spec §8. The engine-owned audio object is self.scene_manager.audio (an AudioEngine); examples bind it to audio.

3.1 The one call [Owner]#

coin = audio.sound('coin')                         # first time: compiled; afterwards: from the cache
blip = audio.sound('blip', pitch='E5')             # each pitch is its own compiled sound
coin.play()                                        # getting never plays; play() does
audio.sound('blip', pitch='E5').play(volume=0.8)   # the same call inline in gameplay: a cache hit
audio.play('coin')                                 # shorthand for audio.sound('coin').play()

audio.sound(...) takes two kinds of argument:

  • What the sound is. The source (a name, a path or an Instrument), plus pitch, notes, chord, length, transpose and parameters. These identify the compiled sound. Different values give a different sound, compiled once each.
  • Nothing about playback. Volume, pan, bus, loop and priority belong to .play(...). A sound file may set defaults for them.

audio.play(source, ..., volume=..., ...) accepts both kinds and splits them: the identity arguments go to sound(), the playback arguments to play().

Where compiling happens is the game's choice. Calling audio.sound(...) in setup compiles before the first frame. The first call during gameplay compiles in that frame. Either way the engine times and counts every compile, and the diagnostics name any that happened inside a gameplay frame (§11.1). setup is the convention, not a separate API.

3.2 Referring to a sound [Owner: "Could we support both formats"]#

The game writes The engine reads it as
'coin' A short name, looked up in the sound files under the scene's SOUND_DIRECTORY (a class attribute like TARGET_FPS; default 'sounds', relative to the game's package)
'sounds/pickups.toml#coin' A path to a sound file plus the name of a [sounds.*] table in it
'sounds/sfx_slap.wav' A path to a recorded sound
Instrument(...) An instrument built in code, used directly
  • A string is a path if it contains /, # or a file extension. Otherwise it is a short name.
  • Short names are indexed the first time one is looked up: every .toml sound file in SOUND_DIRECTORY is read for its [sounds.*] table names. Nothing is compiled by indexing.
  • If two files define the same short name, the first lookup of that name raises SoundAssetError naming both files. A short name never silently picks one.
  • A WAV or OGG file in the directory is also a short name, by its file stem (sfx_slap).
  • Paths are relative to the game's package, as sprite files are [Fact: sprites load from package paths].

W1. Play a basic waveform with sensible defaults#

audio.beep()                                       # A4 square, 0.1 s, pluck envelope, effects bus
audio.beep(pitch='C5', wave='triangle', length=0.25)
audio.beep(pitch=880.0)                            # a float is hertz

beep(...) is audio.sound(Instrument(wave=...), pitch=..., length=...).play(...) in one line. A short beep compiles in well under a millisecond, and it is cached like any other sound.

W2. Get sounds and play them#

class GameScene(Scene):
    SOUND_DIRECTORY = 'sounds'

    def setup(self) -> None:
        audio = self.scene_manager.audio
        # Convention: get every sound once here, so gameplay never compiles
        self.coin = audio.sound('coin')
        self.slap = audio.sound('sfx_slap')
        self.blips = {note: audio.sound('blip', pitch=note) for note in ('C5', 'E5', 'G5')}

    def on_coin_collected(self) -> None:
        self.coin.play()
        self.blips['E5'].play(volume=0.8, pan=-0.3)

A game may equally keep only names and call audio.play('coin') in gameplay. The first such call compiles, and later calls are cache hits.

W3. Instruments, notes and chords [Owner]#

pulse = Instrument(wave='pulse_25', envelope='pluck')               # how it sounds

audio.sound(pulse, pitch='C5')                                      # one note
audio.sound(pulse, notes='B5:0.06 E6:0.18')                         # a melody: the coin
audio.sound(pulse, chord=('C4', 'E4', 'G4'), length=0.5)            # notes together

thick = Instrument(sources={
    'a': Wave('saw'),
    'b': Wave('saw').detuned(cents=7),
    'grit': Noise('white', level=0.1),
})
audio.sound(thick, pitch=Hz(110))                                   # all three layers at 110 Hz
  • notes is a string of pitch:seconds tokens. _:seconds is a rest. It can also be a sequence of (pitch, seconds) pairs, where a pitch may be a note, Hz, Midi or scale step (§4.1). The notes are baked into one sound, sample-accurately.
  • chord plays its pitches together. In a baked sound they are rendered together into one voice's audio.
  • transpose=semitones shifts a notes or chord sound. pitch with notes or chord is a ValueError (use transpose).
  • An unpitched instrument (noise, the CRT static, an explosion) needs no pitch. If given, its pitch scales the instrument's rates (noise rate, filter cutoff) instead of naming a note.
  • Length: a single note or chord lasts length seconds (default 0.25 s) before its release begins. The compiled sound ends after the release tail. A notes sound ends after the last note's release.

W4. One-shot, looping and streamed audio#

audio.play('coin')                                         # one-shot
hum = audio.sound('ambience_hum').play(loop=True)          # loops until stopped
hum.stop(fade=0.5)                                         # graceful: half-second fade
audio.music.play_file('music/title.ogg', loop=True)        # streamed long-form audio (M5)

Streamed audio uses pygame's own streaming player (pygame.mixer.music) [Proposal]. There is one stream at a time, it is never compiled into memory, and it is not a sound().

W5. Update a sound from game state (live sounds, M2)#

# vehicle.toml marks the instrument live and exposes 'rpm' (600-7000) and 'load' (0-1); §9.2
self.engine_sound = audio.sound('vehicle').start(parameters={'rpm': 800.0})

def update(self) -> None:
    self.engine_sound.set('rpm', self.car.rpm)       # smoothed by the instrument
    self.engine_sound.set('load', self.car.throttle)

def on_crash(self) -> None:
    self.engine_sound.release()                      # envelope release, then it ends
  • A live sound uses the same audio.sound(...) call. Compiling it validates it and builds its wave tables; its audio is rendered while it plays.
  • .start(...) returns a live handle. .play() on a live sound starts it with default parameters.
  • A baked sound may also take fixed parameter values, which identify it like a pitch does: audio.sound('thud', parameters={'impact_energy': 0.6}) is its own compiled sound. Values are never rounded to a nearest compiled one; a different value is a different sound.

W6. Melody and a beat-aligned adaptive-music transition (M5)#

calm = Sequence(tempo=96, meter=(4, 4), tracks={
    'lead': Track('chip_lead', notes='E4 G4 A4 _ | G4 E4 D4 _ | C4 D4 E4 G4 | E4 _ _ _'),
    'bass': Track('chip_bass', notes='C2 _ G2 _ | A1 _ E2 _ | F1 _ C2 _ | G1 _ _ _'),
})
audio.music.play(audio.sound(calm, loop_length='auto'))
audio.music.switch_to(audio.sound(tense), at='next_bar')

A Sequence is something you can compile with audio.sound(...). Inside it, tokens are beats, _ holds, | marks bars, and every note is sample-accurate because the sequence is baked. Switching between sequences lands on the requested musical boundary to within one game frame plus one device buffer (§7.3). That is a measured guarantee, not a sample-accurate one.

W7. Route sounds through buses#

audio.buses['music'].volume = 0.4
audio.buses['effects'].mute = True
audio.play('coin', bus='ui')
audio.buses.add('engine', parent='effects', volume=0.8)    # custom bus
audio.buses['music'].duck_under('dialogue', amount_db=-9.0, attack=0.05, release=0.4)   # M5

W8. Inspect voices, compiles and underruns#

report = audio.diagnostics()
report.voices_playing, report.voices_limit          # total and per bus, baked and live
report.compiles                                     # count and milliseconds, setup vs gameplay frames
report.compiles_in_gameplay                         # (name, ms, frame) of each compile inside a gameplay frame
report.live_render_milliseconds                     # p50/p99/max over the last second
report.underruns, report.dropped_plays, report.stolen_voices
report.device                                       # 44100 Hz, s16, stereo, 512 frames, driver

The same numbers are drawn live by the debug overlay's Audio panel (§11.1.1; F3, then F4 to it, or --sound-diagnostics to open on it).

W9. Render a reproducible sound to a file, offline#

from glitchygames.audio import render_to_file

render_to_file(audio.sound('explosion'), 'out/explosion.wav')
render_to_file(audio.sound('vehicle'), 'out/vehicle.wav', parameter_timeline={'rpm': [(0.0, 800), (3.0, 7000)]})

uv run glitchygames sound render sounds/vehicle.toml#vehicle --parameter rpm=800:7000:3s -o vehicle.wav

Offline rendering opens no audio device and runs faster than real time. With the same engine version, platform, configuration, assets and seed, it produces the same samples (§10).

Lifecycle: the four ways a sound ends#

Call Applies to What is heard When the voice is free
handle.release() live sounds and looping baked sounds Live: the envelope's release stage. Looping baked: the loop ends after the current pass and its baked tail plays After the release or tail
handle.stop(fade=0.05) any voice A fade over fade seconds (minimum 5 ms) After the fade
handle.cancel() any voice A 5 ms de-click fade, nothing longer Within 5 ms
handle.unschedule() a play scheduled with at_frame that has not started (M2) Nothing At once. Returns False if it already started

Calls on a handle whose voice has ended, or was stolen, do nothing and return False. Ending a voice twice is harmless. Handles never control a different voice that later reused the slot (implementation spec §4.4: generation counters). release() on a non-looping baked sound does nothing and returns False, because its release is already part of its audio.

Errors#

Situation Behaviour
Audio device unavailable (no mixer) sound() still compiles (so offline rendering and tests work). play() is a silent no-op that returns a handle whose methods return False. One warning is logged (today's SoundBank behaviour [Fact], kept)
Unknown short name KeyError naming the sound directory and close matches
Duplicate short name SoundAssetError naming both files
Missing file in a path SoundAssetError naming the path and where it was resolved from
Malformed sound file SoundAssetError with the file, the TOML field path and the reason
Invalid argument (volume=-1, pan=3, bare integer pitch, pitch with notes) ValueError naming the parameter and its valid range
Unknown parameter on set KeyError naming the instrument's exposed parameters

4. Pitch, waves and sources#

4.1 Pitch: notes and frequencies side by side [Owner]#

Three explicit types name a pitch. Each is unambiguous:

Note('A4')      # note name; sharps 'F#4' and flats 'Bb3'
Hz(440.0)       # frequency
Midi(69)        # MIDI note number

Anywhere a pitch is accepted, a string is a note name and a float is hertz. A bare int is rejected with a message suggesting Midi(60) or Hz(60), because 60 could mean middle C or a 60 Hz hum (explicit is better than implicit).

Moving pitches, in both vocabularies:

Note('C4').up(semitones=7)      # musician: up a fifth
Hz(220).times(1.5)              # anyone: one and a half times higher
Hz(220).octave_up()             # Hz(440)
Note('C4').detune(cents=-8)     # slightly flat

Scales as numbered steps: the off-ramp for people who don't read music [Owner].

scale = Scale('C', 'major_pentatonic')
melody = [(scale.step(n), 0.15) for n in (0, 2, 4, 2, 5)]   # every step is in key
audio.sound(pulse, notes=melody).play()

Scales provided: major, minor, major_pentatonic, minor_pentatonic, blues, chromatic, whole_tone, dorian, mixolydian. A step past the top wraps into the next octave.

Frequency tables stay valid. A game's own NOTE_FREQUENCIES = {'C4': 261.63, ...} (Mr. Muncher, Clockwork Keep [Fact]) keeps working, because it is just a table the game owns. PitchTable.from_dict(...) makes such a table first-class (table['C4'] returns a Hz), so a game can keep thinking in its own names.

Tuning [Proposal]: 12-tone equal temperament with A4 = 440 Hz, set per engine (audio.tuning = Tuning(a4=432.0)). The tuning is part of a compiled sound's identity, so changing it recompiles on the next request. Retro pitch quantization (§8) is a profile option, kept separate from the pitch types.

Ranges. The supported fundamental range is 8 Hz to 12 kHz [Proposal]. Below that, a sound is inaudible as pitch. Above that, a periodic wave's harmonics alias even with band-limiting. Hz accepts 0 to Nyquist (22.05 kHz at the 44.1 kHz mixer rate):

  • Values outside the supported range but inside that one log a warning once per sound.
  • Non-finite values, negatives and values above Nyquist raise ValueError (today's _validate_frequencies rule [Fact], kept).

4.2 Named waves, blends, wavetables and custom waves [Owner]#

Built-in waves by name: sine, triangle, saw, square, pulse_12, pulse_25, pulse_75 (the three non-square duty cycles a classic pulse channel offers), and pulse with an explicit duty from 0.01 to 0.99.

Wave('square')
Wave('pulse', duty=0.125)

buzzy = Wave.mix(square=0.6, triangle=0.4)          # weights, normalized for you
hollow = Wave('saw').blend('sine', amount=0.3)      # 30% of the way to sine

Blend versus layer (the API keeps them distinct):

  • Blend (Wave.mix, blend, WaveTable) produces one oscillator with a new shape. It is cheap, and it is the classic wavetable sound.
  • Layer (several sources in an instrument, §5.1) runs separate oscillators that can be detuned against each other for a thick, chorused sound.

Wavetables (M4):

table = WaveTable(['sine', 'triangle', 'saw', 'square'])
charge = audio.sound(Instrument(wavetable=table, position=Modulated('charge', 0.0, 3.0)),
                     pitch='C4').start()
charge.set('charge', 0.4)

A position of 2.5 is half saw, half square. The position moves continuously and is smoothed (§5.4). Interpolation within a cycle is linear between table samples. Between waves it is a linear crossfade [Proposal].

Custom waves with named numbers [Owner: "give those numbers named constants ... to help explain what's going on"]. Every number in a wave definition is a keyword or a named constant, never a bare positional list:

from glitchygames.audio import Harmonics, Wave, WavePoint, CYCLE, LEVEL

# "How loud is each overtone", by the overtone's musical name
ORGAN = Harmonics(
    fundamental=1.0,        # the note itself
    octave=0.5,             # 2x the frequency
    octave_and_fifth=0.0,   # 3x
    two_octaves=0.25,       # 4x
)
audio.waves.register('organ', Wave.from_harmonics(ORGAN))

# Points across one cycle: where in the cycle (CYCLE) and how high (LEVEL)
GHOST = (
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.START,          level=LEVEL.CENTER),
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.EIGHTH,         level=0.8),            # a sharp rise...
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.QUARTER,        level=0.3),            # ...that sags
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.HALF,           level=LEVEL.CENTER),
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.THREE_QUARTERS, level=-0.6),
    WavePoint(at=CYCLE.END,            level=LEVEL.CENTER),
)
audio.waves.register('ghost', Wave.from_points(GHOST, shape='smooth'))   # or 'linear', 'steps'
Wave.mix(ghost=0.5, square=0.5)
  • Harmonics also takes harmonic_5=... through harmonic_32=... for higher overtones, and Harmonics.drawbars('888000000') for the nine-drawbar organ notation (16', 5⅓', 8', 4', 2⅔', 2', 1⅗', 1⅓', 1'; each digit 0–8). That gives organ players their own names.
  • CYCLE constants are START, EIGHTH, QUARTER, THREE_EIGHTHS, HALF, FIVE_EIGHTHS, THREE_QUARTERS, SEVEN_EIGHTHS and END (0.0–1.0). LEVEL constants are TOP (1.0), HIGH (0.5), CENTER (0.0), LOW (-0.5) and BOTTOM (-1.0). Any float in range works as well.
  • Wave.from_samples(levels=..., resolution_bits=4) takes a fixed-length table, such as the 32-step, 4-bit wave RAM style of a Game Boy-like wave channel (M4).
  • Wave.from_sprite('waves/ghost.toml') reads a Bitmappy sprite (M4). Each pixel column is one step of the cycle, and the topmost lit pixel in that column is the level. Drawing a wave as pixel art is a GlitchyGames-only trick [Proposal] (§13).
  • Registered waves belong to the engine context. A wave name used in a sound file must be registered (in code or in a [waves.*] table of any file under SOUND_DIRECTORY) before the sound is first requested.

4.5 Hybrid instruments#

Up to four sources per instrument (oscillators, wavetables, noise, samples) are mixed with per-source level, pitch offset and envelope. Layered oscillators and noise are available in M1; samples and wavetables join in M4. The canonical example is §9.2's hybrid impact.

5. Instrument behaviour and modulation#

5.1 Instrument structure#

An instrument is:

  1. up to 4 sources, each with an optional envelope;
  2. their mix;
  3. an optional filter with its own envelope;
  4. the amplifier envelope;
  5. effects;
  6. level and pan.

Exposed parameters drive any modulatable value through mapping curves.

5.2 Envelopes#

ADSR has attack, decay, sustain (level 0–1) and release, in seconds. Each stage has a curve: linear, exponential or steps(n) (stepped, chip-style). Named presets cover the common cases: 'pluck', 'pad', 'organ', 'percussive' and 'swell'. The existing Envelope(attack, decay, sustain_level, release) [Fact] keeps working.

Retrigger rules for live notes: retrigger = "restart" (from zero, with a 2 ms de-click), "legato" (keep the current level and glide the pitch) or "from_current" (restart the attack from the current level). The default is "from_current" [Proposal].

5.3 Modulation#

  • LFOs: wave by name (any registered wave), rate in Hz (or note lengths at a tempo, M5), depth, phase and optional per-note retrigger. At most 2 per instrument (M4).
  • Pitch: tuning, detune_cents, pitch bend (in semitones, with the range set per instrument), vibrato (an LFO on pitch) and glide (M4).
  • Pitch curves (M1): a note's pitch can slide to a target over a time with an easing curve. This is today's frequency_curve with easing [Fact].
  • Velocity: 0–1, mapped to level and optionally to filter cutoff.
  • Routes (M2): source → destination × amount.
  • Sources: an envelope, an LFO, velocity, note pitch or an exposed parameter.
  • Destinations: pitch, level, pan, filter cutoff, filter resonance, pulse duty, wavetable position or FM index.
  • At most 8 routes per instrument. Cycles are rejected at load.
  • FM, ring modulation and hard sync are three distinct operations (M4):
  • FM: 2- and 4-operator arrangements chosen from named algorithms (stack, pair, y, branch, …), with optional feedback on one operator (0–1, one-sample delay).
  • Ring modulation: multiplies two sources, for bell-like and metallic tones.
  • Hard sync: one oscillator's cycle restarts another's, for tearing, vocal-like leads.

5.4 Exposed game parameters, mapping and smoothing (M2)#

  • Values outside range are clamped and counted.
  • Smoothing is a one-pole glide in the instrument's own time. A control update can never jump the sound in a way that clicks [Brief].
  • Update rates: the game may call set every frame or more often. Only the latest value per parameter per render chunk is used. Updates are coalesced, never queued without limit.
  • Late updates: a value set during a frame affects audio that has not been rendered yet. That is after the already-queued chunk, so the delay is at most one chunk (§7.1).
  • Expired handles: set on a released or ended live sound returns False and does nothing.

5.5 Filters#

Low-pass, high-pass and band-pass have a cutoff (Hz or a note) and a resonance (0–0.95 of self-oscillation, clamped to stay stable) [Proposal]. Each instrument has one filter, with a filter envelope (M2). M1 sounds use the existing FIR low- and high-pass filters [Fact] without resonance.

6. Voices, buses and mixing#

6.1 Voice limits and categories#

  • Baked voices each use one mixer channel. The default is 32 mixer channels for the sound library [Proposal]. Mixing happens in SDL's C mixer, so baked voices are cheap.
  • Live voices render in Python and keep the reserved live pool [Fact: --live-voice-pool, default 4]. M3 raises the pool to 8 if its frame-time measurements allow (implementation spec §11).
  • Older code that calls Sound.play() directly (SoundBank, load_sound) keeps its own unreserved channels (pygame's default 8), so it never takes a library channel and the library never cuts it off.
  • Category reservations: each bus may reserve a minimum number of mixer channels (ui: 2, music: 2 by default), so a storm of effects can never silence a menu click.

6.2 Voice stealing and overload#

When a play finds no free channel in its bus's share:

  1. Steal the lowest-priority voice, the oldest first among equals, provided its priority is lower than or equal to the new play's. priority runs 0–100; the defaults are 50 for effects, 80 for UI and 90 for music.
  2. A stolen voice fades out over 10 ms instead of cutting off.
  3. If nothing is stealable, the new play is dropped and counted (dropped_plays).

Release tails count toward the limit until they end. A voice in its release stage is preferred for stealing over one at the same priority that is not releasing.

When live voices exceed their render budget (§11):

  1. the engine lowers live-voice quality (filter quality first, then wavetable interpolation);
  2. if that is not enough, it releases the lowest-priority live voice.

Both steps are logged. Virtualizing inaudible voices is deferred.

6.3 Buses#

The default buses are music, effects, ui, ambience and dialogue, under master. Games can add custom buses with a parent. The bus graph is a tree, so it has no cycles.

  • Volume (0–1, or volume_db), mute and solo per bus. Changes apply to voices already playing.
  • Pan per voice, from -1 (left) to +1 (right), with a constant-power law (-3 dB at centre).
  • Fades: bus.fade_to(volume, seconds).
  • Ducking: bus.duck_under(other_bus, amount_db, attack, release) (M5).
  • Bus effects: buses apply volume, pan, ducking and fades in real time. Effects that change the sound itself (echo, bit reduction) belong to instruments and are compiled in (§6.4). Real-time bus effects need the native-core decision (implementation spec, decision D3).

6.5 Headroom and clipping#

  • Compiled sounds keep the author's levels. They are not silently normalized.
  • Compiling reports each sound's peak. A peak above 0 dBFS is clipped to full scale and logged once with the sound's name.
  • normalize = true in a sound (or normalize=True in code) scales the peak to -1 dBFS.
  • A loudness budget per bus (M2) lowers that bus's volume briefly when exceeded, to keep SDL's mixer from hard-clipping during explosion storms. The default budget is a summed peak of at most 1.5 across the bus's playing voices.
  • Meters report each bus's summed peak (an estimate from compiled peaks and volumes; no audio is read back) and the clip-protection events.

6.6 Positional audio (M4)#

.play(position=(x, y)) uses the active camera: pan comes from horizontal offset and attenuation from distance, with a cutoff radius. Nothing beyond 2D is planned.

7. Timing and music#

7.1 Clock domains#

Domain Unit Who uses it Replayed?
Frame integer frame number and the frame time (dt) the engine served [Fact: glitchygames.clock] game code, scheduling, voice bookkeeping, replays Yes
Sound frames integer audio sample frames inside one compiled sound the renderer n/a (deterministic)
Beats beats at a sequence's tempo sequences (M5) n/a
Device the mixer's playback nothing in the public API No

Rules:

  • play() starts the sound at the next mixer buffer after the call. With the 512-frame buffer at 44.1 kHz, that is within about 11.6 ms [Fact: buffer size, CLAUDE.md]. The start is not sample-accurate.
  • Inside a compiled sound, timing is sample-accurate. Every note in notes lands on its exact sample frame, because the whole sound is rendered at once.
  • Live parameter latency: a live voice renders ahead in chunks (default 100 ms today [Fact]; M3 evaluates 50 ms). A parameter change is heard within one chunk plus one device buffer.
  • play(..., at_frame=n) (M2) schedules a play for the start of frame n, deterministically in replays, with frame resolution.
  • Late scheduled plays (M2): a play scheduled for a frame that has already passed starts now if it is at most late_tolerance_frames late (default 2). Otherwise it is dropped. Either way it is counted.

7.2 Sequences (M5)#

A sequence has a tempo (BPM), a meter, optional tempo changes at bar boundaries, and tracks of note events: pitch, start beat, length in beats, velocity, and optional parameter changes. It can be written three ways:

  • note strings ('E4 G4 A4 _ |', one beat per token);
  • (pitch, beats) pairs, where a pitch is a note, Hz, Midi or scale step;
  • a step grid for drums ('x . . x | x . x .').

Beats are converted to seconds through the tempo map, then to sound frames, rounded to the nearest frame per event. Each event is converted from its absolute beat, so rounding never accumulates. M1's notes= string (seconds per token) is the simple form for jingles.

7.3 Music transport (M5)#

  • The transport offers audio.music.play(sound), stop(fade), pause(), resume(), switch_to(sound, at='now' | 'next_beat' | 'next_bar' | 'end', crossfade), and layer control (audio.music.layer('drums').volume = 0).
  • Layers are sequences compiled with identical length and tempo and started in the same frame, so they stay aligned. Whether their starts land in the same mixer buffer is verified by measurement.
  • Boundary accuracy: transitions are scheduled from the transport's own count of the current music's playback, on the frame clock. They land within one frame plus one device buffer of the exact boundary [Proposal, to be measured in M5]. Sample-accurate transitions are deferred to the native-core decision (D3).
  • Pause: see §7.4. Already-scheduled plays keep their frame numbers.
  • Time scaling (slow motion): compiled sounds are not resampled. A bus may be marked follows_time_scale, and new plays on it then use a pitch- and speed-scaled compiled sound (M5, optional).
  • Seeking within music is supported only for streamed files (pygame.mixer.music.set_pos).

7.4 Scenes, pause, replays and shutdown#

  • Scene switches: voices belong to the scene that started them, like live voices today [Fact: LiveAudio.update(active_scene=...)]. A scene's voices stop with a 50 ms fade when it is cleaned up, unless played with persist=True. Music persists by default. Compiled sounds stay cached across scenes.
  • Pause screens: a scene behind a pause scene has its voices paused (Channel.pause) and resumed later (today's live-voice rule [Fact]). A paused voice keeps its channel and its place in the sound.
  • Replays and instant replay: sounds started in silent rebuild or fast-forward frames are stopped (the existing FrameSilencer [Fact]). Baked and live voices go through the same mixer channels, so the silencer covers them.
  • Recordings: plays are not recorded as input. They are reproduced because the game's logic reproduces them.
  • Shutdown: all voices fade within 50 ms. The engine waits at most 100 ms, then quits the mixer.

8. Retro profiles (M4)#

Profiles constrain or configure the one engine. They are authoring presets and validation rules, not separate playback systems [Brief].

Profile What it constrains Feels like
Chip Waves limited to pulse (12.5/25/50/75% duty), triangle (4-bit stepped) and LFSR noise; envelopes in steps(16); pitch quantized to a period register (see below); at most 4 simultaneous voices per sound family 8-bit consoles
Wavetable 32-sample, 4-bit custom waves; no filter; volume in 4 steps handheld wave channels
FM 2- or 4-operator algorithms only; sine operators; feedback on one operator 16-bit FM consoles and arcade boards
Sampler Samples resampled at compile time to a reduced rate (e.g. 16 kHz) with an 8-bit option, optional gaussian-like smoothing and echo 16-bit sample-based consoles
Hybrid No restrictions; any of the above together modern "retro-inspired"

Pitch quantization (Chip profile): the frequency snaps to what an integer period divider of a reference clock can produce (frequency = clock / (16 × (period + 1)), with the clock and period bits as profile parameters). This makes high notes slightly out of tune in the authentic way.

The classic palettes as authoring inspiration (descriptions of character, not emulation targets):

  • C64-style (SID): three voices, each a saw, triangle, pulse or noise wave, with ring modulation, hard sync and one shared resonant multimode filter. Buzzy, filter-swept leads and basses.
  • NES-style: two pulse channels with four duty cycles, a stepped triangle (bass, no volume control), and LFSR noise in long and short modes. Crisp, clean, rhythmic.
  • Game Boy-style: two pulses (one with a pitch sweep), a 32-step 4-bit wave channel and LFSR noise, hard-panned in stereo. Thin and characterful.
  • Genesis-style: 4-operator FM with feedback (6 channels) plus a simple square-wave PSG. Metallic basses, brassy stabs, punchy drums.
  • SNES-style: 8 sampled voices with compressed samples, soft gaussian interpolation and a long echo. Lush, slightly muffled, reverberant.

The engine does not advertise cycle-accurate emulation; hardware emulation would be a separate project. No recordings, ROM-extracted samples or proprietary instrument banks are included. Every preset in the engine is authored from scratch.

9.3 Validation, versions, paths and the cache#

  • Validation happens when a file is first read, never during play. Each error names the file, the TOML path (instruments.vehicle.routes[1].output), the value and what was expected.
  • Unknown fields are errors in files with a header, so typos are caught. In version 0 recipe files they are warnings.
  • Versions: a file with a newer version than the engine understands is refused, naming the engine version needed. Older versions are migrated in memory, in order (0→1→…), each step a tested function.
  • Paths (samples, sprites, other sound files) are relative to the file that names them. Paths that escape the game's package (../../) are refused.
  • Missing assets raise when the sound that needs them is first requested, naming the file and the referring field.
  • Authoritative data: the TOML files, WAV samples and sprites. Generated, disposable data: compiled audio in memory and the optional disk cache.
  • The engine-owned cache is a tri-state [Owner: "cache=None, cache='disk', cache='ram'"]:
Mode --sound-cache What happens
None none Nothing is kept. Every play() compiles its sound again, in the frame that plays it. This is the switch that forces mid-frame rendering, for measurement and A/B tests
'ram' ram (default) Compiled audio is kept in memory, keyed by a hash of everything that identifies the sound: the resolved instrument, pitch or notes, parameters, tuning, sample rate, seed, the engine's audio render version and the numpy version
'disk' disk (M3) The 'ram' behaviour, plus a disk layer in the user cache directory (~/Library/Caches/glitchygames/sounds/ on macOS), so repeat launches skip compiling. The disk layer is size-limited (default 64 MB), safe to delete and never shipped
  • Today's code does not get the flag. The games move to the new API and the old paths are removed first (M2), and then the benchmarks run in order: cache off, then ram, then disk (M3) [Owner: "complete the API migration to clean everything up, then we benchmark with the cache off, then the others"].
  • In code: AudioEngine(cache=None | 'ram' | 'disk'), taken from the option.
  • In the browser and in tests, 'disk' falls back to 'ram' with one log line.
  • The option is local to a run, so a recording replays under any mode.
  • Hot reload (development; --sound-hot-reload, M4): editing a file recompiles the affected sounds on their next request. Voices already playing finish with the old audio.
  • Sound files cannot run code.

10. Determinism and offline rendering#

  • Guarantee: a compiled sound or an offline render produces bit-identical samples for a fixed engine version, Python and numpy version, platform, configuration, asset set, seed and parameter timeline. Bit identity across platforms is not promised; cross-platform comparisons use a tolerance (implementation spec §9).
  • Noise and any randomness in a sound come from the sound's own seed (§4.3), never from game streams.
  • Offline rendering uses the same compile path:
  • An offline mix of an event list ([(seconds, sound, pan, volume), ...]) places each sound at its exact sample offset, which is more precise than the device path.
  • Output is WAV, 16-bit or float32, at a chosen rate, with an optional stem per bus.
  • Tails end when the output stays below -90 dBFS for 100 ms, or at tail_seconds, whichever comes first.
  • Clipping in offline output is reported, never silently saturated (float32 output keeps it).

11.3 Device and failure behaviour#

  • Unavailable audio: the game runs silent, with one warning, and play() is a no-op [Fact: current behaviour, kept].
  • Device removed or added: the engine already receives AUDIODEVICEADDED/REMOVED [Fact: src/glitchygames/events/audio.py]. When the output device is removed, voices stop. If SDL moves to the new default device, play continues. Otherwise the engine reopens the mixer with the same format on the next play. Compiled audio is kept in Python memory, so nothing is recompiled unless the rate changed (M2).
  • Suspend/resume: live voices that underrun restart on the next update [Fact: today's rule].
  • Shutdown: see §7.4.

12. Showing it off: demos, games and the CRT#

12.1 CRT power-on sound (Milestone 1) [Owner]#

The sound follows the existing 1.2 s sequence [Fact: SEQUENCE_SECONDS = 1.2 in crt_power_on.py]. The cue times are fractions of the sequence's own progress, the same progress that drives the picture:

Progress Picture [Fact] Sound [Proposal]
0.00 black The mechanical click of the power switch, then the low thoom of the degauss coil: a 60 Hz hum with its harmonics, swelling and fading over ~0.5 s
dot → line the beam dot stretches The flyback whine fades in: a thin, high tone, pitched lower than a real set's 15.7 kHz so more people hear it. A constant sets the pitch
picture opens the picture opens from the line A burst of static crackle that thins out through the warm-up flicker
background settles test pattern or channel 3 Test pattern: the classic 1 kHz bars tone. Channel 3: a soft hiss. Either is quiet, and fades out over 0.5 s once the game's first frame is shown
any key the sequence skips Everything fades out over 20 ms, with no click

Rules (the power-on's existing rules [Fact: module docstring], extended to sound):

  • It plays only when the picture does (should_play).
  • It never plays under --headless, in tests, or before replays, audits, rebaselines or recordings.
  • It uses no game random stream; the static has its own fixed seed.
  • It changes no game state or clock.
  • --crt-power-on-sound off silences it without removing the picture [Proposal].
  • The engine compiles these sounds before the sequence starts. They are short and compile in a few milliseconds.
  • They compile once per process and every later power-on reuses them, in any engine context (the Arcade), whatever the sound cache mode [Owner] (impl spec 10.1).
  • CRT power-off: a falling whine and a soft thump, for when the collapse animation is built (plans/future-features.md #53). It is specified with that feature.

13. More interesting applications [Proposal; the owner asked to keep exploring]#

These are ideas to evaluate, not commitments. Each names the milestone that would make it possible.

  1. VCR sounds for the channel 3 screen and instant replay. Tape whir and a head click when the channel 3 screen appears, a rewind squeal while instant replay rewinds (src/glitchygames/instant_replay.py [Fact]), and wow-and-flutter on audio during replay playback (M4).
  2. The arcade's chime over the colour bars. plans/future-features.md #43 [Fact] asks for "an original chime over the bars". It becomes a sound in the engine's own sound file (M1).
  3. A recording's soundtrack. Plays are deterministic consequences of game logic, so the engine can log them per frame and render a recording's audio offline. That is the audio.raw capture that plans/future-work.md §"Headless capture" asks for [Fact: it notes the mixer cannot be tapped], and it gives video exports sound (M5).
  4. Sprites that sing. Wave.from_sprite turns any pixel art into a wave, so a game's own sprites can become its instruments. The ghost sprite's outline is the ghost's voice (M4).
  5. The controller speaker as a bus. A controller bus routes chosen sounds to a DualSense's own speaker through the existing ControllerAudio [Fact], for a radio voice in your hands (M5; miniaudio stays scoped to controller audio).
  6. Rumble that matches the sound. The same envelope drives controller rumble, so a thud's impact and its vibration share one curve (M2; the engine has rumble devices [Fact: scene.py _rumble_devices]).
  7. A Geiger counter for frame time (a developer tool). Each slow frame clicks, GC pauses buzz, and the frame-time p99 becomes a pitch. Debugging performance by ear (M2).
  8. A random sound-effect generator. A "make me a pickup / laser / explosion / jump" button in the Sound Board, with seeded randomization and export to TOML. Every result is reproducible from its seed (M4).
  9. A robot voice for text-to-speech. Bit reduction and ring modulation on TTS output (src/glitchygames/audio/tts.py [Fact]) for in-game announcers (M4).
  10. Music from game state. In Drawing Fire or Paddle Slap, rally length drives the beat's intensity layer, so the score follows the game (M5).

16.2 Does caching make gameplay smoother? Measured, after the migration [Owner]#

The owner asked for the sound to be "fully benchmarked and measured" with plots in the docs, for A/B tests that show the cache's effect "in a really clear way", and for the cache to be a feature flag "so we can test with it on and off to force mid-frame audio rendering". The order is the owner's: finish the API and migrate every game onto it, removing the old paths (M1–M2); then benchmark with the cache off; then ram; then disk (M3).

The "before" numbers already exist. The replay ledger measures every golden replay and bundled demo at every commit [Fact: docs/quality/performance.md, quality/replay-ledger/]. The commit before the migration is therefore the baseline of today's code, and the commit after it shows the migration's own effect, with no flag added to code that is about to be removed (AUD-MEAS-007).

The switch. --sound-cache none | ram | disk (§9.3). With none, every play compiles its sound in the frame that plays it. Nothing else differs between runs.

Per-frame audio accounting (M1). Every compile, file decode and live chunk is timed with the engine's raw counter [Fact: clock.raw_counter_ns, measurement only]. Each recorded frame gets two measurement fields, which like processing_seconds are never compared or served [Fact: recording_history.MEASUREMENT_FRAME_FIELDS]:

  • audio_seconds: the frame's total audio work;
  • audio_work: what that work was (kind, sound, microseconds).

A frame's slowdown under none can then be explained exactly, not just observed.

Three A/B tests, from clearest to most realistic (M3):

  1. Each sound's cost. Every sound the five migrated games use, compiled under pytest-benchmark, plus one live-voice chunk. That gives one bar per sound.
  2. The sound storm. A stand-in scene does a fixed amount of work per frame and plays k sounds per frame from a scripted schedule (k = 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, plus bursts), for 600 headless unpaced frames under each mode. The frames are identical except for the cache, so the difference in frame time is the audio work. The chart shows the cost rising with k under none and staying flat under ram and disk.
  3. The real games. Every golden replay and bundled demo of a game with sound is rebaselined under each mode on the same machine and commit [Fact: --rebaseline records per-frame processing_seconds; --compare-processing compares]. The plots overlay the runs frame by frame and mark the frames that played a sound.

disk adds a fourth measurement: time from launch to the first frame, cold (empty cache) versus warm. The audio glitch probe [Fact: scripts/audio_glitch_probe.py] runs under each mode too.

The plots go on a new docs page, Sound Performance (docs/quality/sound_performance.md), built like the replay performance page [Fact: docs/quality/performance.md, bokeh charts from committed result rows]:

  • what each sound costs to compile (bars, grouped by game, with 1 ms and 16.7 ms reference lines);
  • frame time against sounds per frame, per mode (the storm), with frames over budget;
  • per-recording frame timelines, none against ram, with audio work shaded and sound frames marked;
  • frame-time distributions (ECDF) per mode;
  • live-voice chunk cost;
  • startup time, cold against warm disk;
  • before and after the migration (from the replay ledger);
  • each benchmark's p99 by commit, so later milestones show up as regressions or gains.

Today's numbers set expectations [Measured]. The worst existing recipe takes 9.7 ms, over half a 60 fps frame if rendered mid-game, and both recipe games prerender at setup today. The none runs will show exactly what that work costs in a frame, and the ram and disk runs what caching saves.
