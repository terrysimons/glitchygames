# Language Arc: Irish (Gaeilge, `ga`)

- **Date:** 2026-11-23
- **Status:** Brainstorm draft. Nothing here is approved for building; it is material to react
  to and to try in small demos.
- **Master plan:** [2026-11-23-spare-credits-exploration.md](2026-11-23-spare-credits-exploration.md)
  (the brainstorm's decisions; the conversation record sits beside it).
- **Language:** Irish (Gaeilge, ISO 639-1 `ga`), the first language arc.

Marking: **(user)** the user decided it; **(my call, proposed)** Claude's proposal, open to
change; **(verify)** a fact still to be checked against a primary source before it ships;
unmarked text explains or connects them.

## How this document works

The arc is written twice over. Each part below first states a **template**: the
language-neutral role that part plays in the game, as a question any language's arc must answer.
Then it gives the **Irish** answer. **(user)** The design could in theory work for any language,
Japanese included. A later language gets its own file with the same parts, named
`2026-11-23-spare-credits-exploration-1-language-arc-<code>.md`, where `<code>` is the
language's ISO 639 code.

The game's shape, from the master plan, which every arc fits into **(user)**:

- A 16-bit-era console RPG in feel (story, party, exploration, grand music, cutscenes, drama),
  with its own look; not a remake of any game.
- The player acquires the language through immersion in a simulated world, not through study
  drills. It should feel fun, even addictive, with the right amount of tension and frustration
  against success and exhilaration.
- A time loop carries the repetition: the player replays, and each pass understands more. The
  loop is a pragmatic, functional part of the world, and the first feature to use AI as an
  integral part.
- A dynamic skill tree with no fixed nodes; language discovery is organic. Its contents are the
  user's to explain.
- The language is spoken and written, with its own script.

## 1. The language and its script

**Template:** which language, how endangered it is today, how many people use it daily, what
scripts it has had, and which dialects exist.

**Irish:**

- **Status today:** UNESCO lists Irish as "definitely endangered". In the 2022 census about 1.87
  million people (40%) said they can speak Irish, but only about 72,000 use it daily outside
  school, and that number is falling while stated ability rises. The gap between "can" and
  "does" is itself a story about a language that is known of but not lived in.
- **Scripts:**
  - **Ogham:** the oldest script, cut as notches along the edge of standing stones (from roughly
    the 4th century).
  - **The Gaelic script (cló Gaelach):** used for print until the mid-20th century; its dot
    above a consonant marked lenition, later written as an `h`.
  - **The Latin alphabet:** the modern standard.

  Three scripts give the game layers of discovery: ogham on the oldest stones, the Gaelic script
  in older books and signs, the modern spelling in living speech. **(verify)** the exact date
  the Gaelic script left print.
- **Dialects:** Munster, Connacht and Ulster, with a written standard (An Caighdeán Oifigiúil).
  Learners trip over dialect differences; label every voiced line with its dialect. **(my call,
  proposed)** Each region of the game world speaks its own dialect, so the dialects are
  geography the player travels through rather than inconsistency.

## 2. The wound

**Template:** the catastrophe that broke the language's transmission from parents to children,
told truthfully, and how the game names it.

**Irish:** **(user)** The Great Hunger of 1845-1852, which the user holds was not a famine but a
massacre.

What the historical record shows, so the story stands on it:

- **Agreed by historians:**
  - The potato blight was natural, but the scale of death was not inevitable.
  - Government policy made it far worse:
    - relief shifted to local rates that collapsed in the west;
    - soup kitchens closed in August 1847;
    - the quarter-acre clause refused relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre unless
      they gave up their land;
    - food continued to leave the country.
  - State relief over five years was a small fraction of what the Crimean War cost.
- **The toll:** about 1 million deaths (estimates range from 775,000 to 1.5 million), mostly from
  disease. At least 1 million emigrated. The population fell from 8.2 million in 1841 to 6.6
  million in 1851, hardest in the west.
- **Contested:** whether there was intent, which is what "genocide" or "massacre" claims. Most
  academic historians reject the genocide label. Writers such as Tim Pat Coogan argue for it,
  and New Jersey's curriculum teaches it so. The radical John Mitchel wrote at the time that
  God sent the blight but the English created the famine.
- **The language:** the poorest districts, the heaviest losses and the most Irish-speaking
  communities overlapped. The 1851 census, the first to count Irish speakers, found about 1.5
  million, against an estimated 3-4 million before. Afterward came silence: families did not
  speak of it, and parents chose English for their children so they could emigrate.

**(my call, proposed)** How the game names it: outsiders and the occupier's records call it "the
Famine", a natural misfortune. The people's own names are **An Gorta Mór** (the Great Hunger)
and **An Drochshaol** (the bad life, the bad times). Recovering those names, and with them the
truth that the hunger was made, is part of what the player rediscovers. The word "famine" is the
corrupted telephone-game version of events. **(user)** The fiction's stand-in empire may be
openly and deliberately cruel; it is a fantasy world.

## 3. The occupier

**Template:** the power whose rule pushed the language down, as an in-world stand-in that evokes
it without naming it, and its villain.

**Irish:** **(user)** Centuries of militaristic occupation by the British. The vibe is an
imperial army with its barracks, and a cruel, zealous commander who evokes Cromwell, never
referenced directly, the way the film Kneecap evokes real people and movements through
stand-ins.

**(my call, proposed)** Stand-ins (working names):

- **The Dominion:** the occupying empire across the water, with garrisons, star forts, barracks
  and a bureaucracy that measures everything.
- **The Lord Protractor:** its commander, a pun on Cromwell's title and on the surveyor's tool.
  He believes the land is disorderly until it is measured, mapped and renamed. Zealous,
  sermonizing, sure of his providence: the cruelty of a man who thinks he is tidying. **(my
  call, proposed)** Kept distinct from the Lord Protector in Cartoon Saloon's *Wolfwalkers*
  (2020), which already used a Cromwell figure. Ours is defined by measuring and renaming, not
  by hunting wolves.
- **The Survey:** the Dominion's project to map the land and replace its names. It draws on the
  1830s Ordnance Survey, which turned Irish place names into English spellings. Historians
  dispute how destructive that really was; Brian Friel's play *Translations* dramatized it as
  loss. The game takes the play's view in fiction.
- **The Relief Commission:** the satire target. A bureaucracy of forms, schedules and clauses;
  for example, a form you may only fill in after surrendering your land.

The record the stand-ins draw on (death tolls and land figures vary by source; use ranges):

- **Conquest in waves:**
  - Norman castles from 1169.
  - The Tudor conquest, including the Munster famine of the 1580s and the scorched-earth
    campaign in Ulster after Kinsale in 1601.
  - The smashing of the O'Neill inauguration stone in 1602.
  - The Flight of the Earls in 1607, and the plantations that followed.
- **The Cromwellian campaign (1649-53):**
  - The sieges of Drogheda and Wexford: a summons, a refusal, then no quarter. Historians
    dispute how many civilians died, and whether deliberately.
  - "To Hell or to Connacht": the transplantation west across the Shannon.
  - Land in Catholic hands falling from about 60% to well under a quarter.
  - Tens of thousands sold into indenture in the Caribbean.
  - Bounties on priests and on wolves.
  - *Mallacht Chromail ort*, "the curse of Cromwell on you", remembered in folklore as the worst
    curse. Historians disagree on how much of that memory formed in the 19th century.
- **The Penal Laws:**
  - A broken treaty (Limerick, 1691).
  - Hedge schools taught in secret, with a child posted as lookout.
  - Mass said at rocks in the open.
  - A 1737 law against using Irish in court.
- **Lasting traces:**
  - Evicted houses with the roofs pulled off.
  - Informers.
  - A network of some 1,600 constabulary barracks by 1901.
  - Constables never posted to their home county.
- **The 20th century:** the Black and Tans, partition, and the Troubles, held lightly (part 11).
- **An irony the story can use:** the 1366 Statutes of Kilkenny punished the occupier's own
  settlers for taking up the Irish language and customs.

**(my call, proposed)** Scenes and systems these give the game:

- **The Lord Protractor's dispatches:** pious letters justifying each atrocity as providence.
  They can be read as found documents.
- **The siege protocol:** summons, refusal, no quarter.
- **The river boundary:** ringed with forts, crossed only with a permit, beyond which the
  dispossessed are sent.
- **The bounty office:** pays for priests and wolves at the same table, a satire beat played
  dark.
- **The smashed inauguration stone:** a restorable relic.
- **The hedge school:** the lookout child warns of patrols, a stealth-and-lesson set piece.
- **The Mass rock:** a hidden gathering place.
- **The Dominion's soldiers are forbidden to learn the language,** after the Statutes of
  Kilkenny. The survey clerk companion's arc is that he learns it anyway.

**Staying distinct from *Wolfwalkers*:** that film already has a Cromwell-like "Lord Protector"
who clears forests and hunts wolves, set in a woodcut-style town. This arc avoids:

- the forest-against-town frame;
- wolves as the stand-in for the people;
- the title "Lord Protector" itself;
- a fall to the villain's death.

It centers instead on language, maps, bureaucracy and garrisons. **(my call, proposed)** "Lord
Protractor" is a deliberate wink at the title. If it sits too close, the user can swap it.

*Kneecap* is the model for handling the stand-ins: the language is living and defiant, and the
figure of the old struggle is ambiguous rather than a hero or a villain.

## 4. The corruption

**Template:** how outsiders' knowledge of the language and its history became garbled, an unkind
game of telephone, and how the player meets and corrects it.

**Irish:** real material for the telephone game:

- **Place names:** an Irish name meant something ("the ford of the hurdles", "the hill of the
  hazel"). Anglicized, it kept only a sound with no meaning. In the old place-lore
  (dinnseanchas), every name carried a story; the map froze them into meaningless spellings.
- **Surnames:** many families dropped the Ó and Mac from their surnames.
- **Words that leaked into English with their meaning lost:**
  - solid etymologies: whiskey (from uisce beatha, "water of life") and banshee (from bean sí,
    "woman of the fairy mound");
  - contested ones (smithereens, galore, phoney) also make good corrupted lore.
- **The ogham "tree alphabet":** a modern myth. The popular claim that every ogham letter is a
  tree comes mostly from Robert Graves in 1948; at most about 8 of the 20 letter names are trees.
  A perfect corrupted-lore item for the player to debunk.
- **Hiberno-English keeps Irish grammar:** "I'm after eating" (I have just eaten), "Have you
  change on you?" The language survives inside the English that replaced it, unrecognized.

**(my call, proposed)** Mechanic: the Dominion's gazetteer, travelers and even well-meaning
scholars hand the hero "facts" that are partly wrong. Tension comes from finding out which, by
checking them against the few real speakers and the old stones. A corrected name restores the
place's story.

## 5. The denial

**Template:** how the hurt shows in the descendants of the people who lost the language, and how
the hero perceives it.

**Irish:** **(user)** The pain of the loss, a hurt to the psyche, means that even descendants of
the displaced deny the truth of what happened to them. In the game this shows as animosity: a
dark cloud forming around a character the hero has a deep emotional connection to.

Real grounding: shame about the language (associated with poverty and backwardness), parents
refusing to pass it on, and silence about the Hunger in families. Folklorists in 1945 found
memories people had not spoken of.

**(my call, proposed)**

- **The cloud reacts to language:** the right word, said gently at the right moment, thins it;
  a clumsy or wrong one thickens it. Learning becomes a way of reaching someone.
- **The phrasing is literal:** in Irish, feelings are "on" you. *Tá brón orm*, "sorrow is on
  me", and *tá fuacht air*, "cold is on him". The cloud is the language's own grammar made
  visible: sorrow sitting on a person, something that can be lifted, not something they are.

## 6. The fading

**Template:** what the world loses as the language fades, shown so the player feels it.

**Irish:** **(user)** One example: a vignette or in-engine video of time passing, the language
fading and the color fading from the world with it. One idea among others, to play with in
demos.

**(my call, proposed)**

- **The prologue as an exact in-engine replay:** a living village in full color and full speech,
  with time passing over it:
  - barracks rise;
  - the blight comes and lazy-bed ridges are abandoned on the hills;
  - famine roads are built that lead nowhere;
  - ships leave.

  Voices thin, color drains, and the last speaker falls silent. The engine already records and
  replays play exactly, so the vignette can be authored as a recording.
- **The colors are Irish words:** Irish divides color differently from English. *Glas* covers
  the green and grey of living things (grass, the sea, stone), *uaine* a vivid green, *liath* the
  grey of age, *gorm* blue, *rua* the red of a fox or of hair, against *dearg* for blood-red.
  **(verify)** these ranges against a dictionary.

  A gray world is *liath*, aged. Learning *glas* returns the living green-grey of field and sea.
  The player literally comes to see the world the way the language sorts it, the "completely
  different way of seeing the world" from the master plan.

## 7. The hero and their attunement

**Template:** who the hero is, what ties them to the language, and how they sense what others
cannot.

**Irish:** **(user)** The hero has a deep emotional connection to their ancestry (backstory
later) and is attuned to "sense", loosely: through sight, hearing others' thoughts, magic, and
so on. That attunement weaves story and mechanics together.

**(my call, proposed)** A working starting point, to be replaced by the user's backstory:

- **The hero:** a descendant of emigrants, raised in the gray, who has inherited a few words
  they don't know the meaning of: a lullaby, a blessing, a curse said under the breath.
- **The person under the cloud:** a grandparent who refuses to speak of the past.
- **The attunement:** the hero hears what others leave unsaid, as half-heard Irish under their
  English. Garbled at first, it comes into focus as the player learns.

**(user)** How the hero first comes to all of this is the user's "one very specific idea", still
to be told. This part keeps a slot for it.

## 8. The loop

**Template:** why time repeats in this world, what bounds one loop, and which of the culture's
own stories the loop stands on.

**Irish:** **(user)** The loop is a pragmatic, functional part of the world, and the first
feature to use AI as an integral part (its role is to be researched, tested and brainstormed).

Irish myth already holds the shapes:

- **Echtra Nerai (The Adventure of Nera):** a medieval time-loop template. On Samhain night Nera
  enters the otherworld, sees the destruction that will come by next Samhain, and returns to
  warn the king in time.
- **Oisín and Tír na nÓg:** about 3 years felt in the otherworld are about 300 passed at home.
  When he touches the ground, the years come down on him.
- **The Children of Lir:** 900 years as swans across three places, keeping their human voices.
  They age the moment the spell breaks.
- **The Tuatha Dé Danann:** they withdrew under the hills behind a magic mist (féth fíada) when
  the land was divided: the surface to the newcomers, the underworld to them.
- **The Morrígan:** the washer at the ford, who foretells the end of the world.

**(my call, proposed)**

- **One loop runs from Samhain to Samhain:** the night the otherworld stands open, as in Nera's
  tale. The hero sees what the year will bring and returns to the start knowing more.
- **The swans and the washer:** the Children of Lir as keepers who remember across loops. The
  washer at the ford as the figure who shows the hero what is coming.
- **(verify)** "The veil is thinnest at Samhain" is a modern phrasing; medieval texts say the
  fairy mounds stood open. Use the older idea.

**(user)** What carries over from one loop to the next is deferred question 15; the user
answers it later.

## 9. The different way of seeing

**Template:** the features of the language that frame the world differently from the player's
own language, and how each can become something the player does.

**Irish:**

| Feature | What it says about the world | **(my call, proposed)** in play |
|---------|------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Feelings are "on" you (*tá brón orm*) | A state sits on a person; it is not who they are | Status effects are phrased this way; the cloud is a weight that can lift |
| Possession is "at" you (*tá peann agam*, "a pen is at me") | You hold things; they are not part of you | Items and even knowledge are held, can be passed on, can be lost |
| No words for yes and no; you echo the verb (*An gcloiseann tú?* / *Cloisim.*) | Answers commit to the action itself | Dialogue answers are verbs; a wrong echo is a misunderstanding |
| Verb first (verb-subject-object) | The happening comes before who did it | Commands and spells start with the action |
| *Is* for what something is, *tá* for its current state | Identity and condition are separate | The occupier can change your state, never what you are |
| Initial mutations (lenition, eclipsis) | Words change their first sound by their neighbors; the change remembers sounds the language lost long ago | A word visibly and audibly shifts in context; recognizing it is a skill |
| *Scáth*: both shadow and shelter (*ar scáth a chéile a mhaireann na daoine*, people live in one another's shelter) | Shade and protection are one idea | Party and companion mechanics; a seed for the multiplayer thinking |
| *Meitheal*: neighbors gathering unasked to work together | Help is communal, not traded | A gathering mechanic: calling a meitheal to rebuild what the loop restores |
| Place-lore (dinnseanchas) | Every name carries a story | Restoring a true place name unlocks the place's story and its color |

**(verify)** each example against a dictionary or grammar before it ships.

## 10. The plot

**Template:** the arc in acts, from the world as the player first finds it to what changes by
the end. Every act names the language beat (what the player comes to understand) beside the
story beat.

**Irish:** **(my call, proposed)** A first draft to react to. The user's specific idea, the
backstory and the deferred questions will reshape it.

### Prologue: The Fading

- **Story:** the in-engine replay of part 6: a living village fades to gray over two hundred
  years in two minutes. It ends on the present-day gray town and the hero.
- **Language:** the player hears Irish, full and musical, before understanding any of it, then
  hears it thin out. The loss lands before the lesson.

### Act I: The Gray Town

- **Story:**
  - A garrison town under the Dominion, everything *liath*.
  - The hero senses the cloud around their grandparent, who mutters a word and denies saying
    it.
  - The Survey is renaming the last unmapped valley.
  - Inciting incident: reserved for the user's specific idea.
  - By the end the hero is caught in the loop at Samhain.
- **Language:** a handful of words the hero half-knows: a greeting, a blessing, their own
  family name with its Ó restored. The first color returns with the first understood word.

### Act II: The Speakers at the Edge

- **Story:**
  - The hero travels west to the islands and the coast, where a few old speakers remain:
    - wary of outsiders;
    - mocked by the Dominion;
    - speaking a dialect the hero's half-words don't match.
  - Companions join (working sketches, all open):
    - a storyteller (seanchaí) who remembers the place-lore;
    - a hedge-school teacher, a fugitive under the Dominion's laws;
    - a keening woman whose laments are the oldest songs;
    - a young person about to emigrate;
    - a Dominion survey clerk whose maps start to trouble him.
  - The Survey's gazetteer is the corrupted lore the party corrects.
- **Language:** listening becomes understanding across loops. The player learns to answer
  without yes or no, and to hear mutations. Calling a meitheal rebuilds a village between loops.

### Act III: An Drochshaol

- **Story:**
  - The loop opens onto the bad times. The Relief Commission's forms and clauses are played as
    bitter satire aimed at the bureaucracy.
  - The hunger itself is played straight:
    - famine roads as dungeons that lead nowhere;
    - the hungry grass as a cursed patch you must carry bread to cross;
    - the walk for relief and the walk back.
  - Real kindness from afar appears too, in a stand-in for the Choctaw Nation's gift of 1847.
- **Language:** the player recovers the true names, An Gorta Mór and An Drochshaol, and with
  them the truth that the hunger was made. The grief words; the keening.

### Act IV: The Lord Protractor

- **Story:**
  - The commander's star fort, where the Survey's master map is kept.
  - The confrontation is more than combat: restoring the true names of the land unmakes the
    Survey's map, and the Protractor's measuring has nothing left to hold.
  - Back home, the hero speaks to the grandparent in Irish. The cloud lifts, and the
    grandparent answers, the first words in decades.
- **Language:** the player says something whole and true in Irish that the game did not hand
  them, built from what they acquired.

### Epilogue: Glas

- **Story:**
  - Color returns as *glas*, the living green-grey.
  - The present day: young speakers, schools teaching through Irish, music.
  - Honest, not triumphant: the language lives, but daily speakers are still few.
- **Language:** the game points outward, to real places to keep learning.

## 11. Tone

**Template:** how the arc holds humor and grief together.

**Irish:**

- **(user)** Tongue-in-cheek, nudge-nudge-wink-wink stand-ins, while being super respectful and
  mindful of the real tragedy. The Troubles are in view as well.
- **(user)** The humor aims at power (the Dominion, the Lord Protractor, the Relief Commission's
  paperwork), never at the people who suffered; the grief is played straight. Proposed by
  Claude; the user confirmed it as "exactly the vibe".
- **(user)** *The Boondock Saints* as a source of humor: distinctly Irish, Boston-Irish humor that
  feels Irish yet unique. Diaspora humor belongs in this arc too, since the hero descends from
  emigrants. **(my call, proposed)** What to take from it: banter between siblings and old
  friends, profane affection, Catholic imagery and ritual played with a wink, and a family
  prayer or saying that becomes a battle cry. Its vigilante violence is not the model; the
  game's confrontations are about words.
- **(my call, proposed)** The Troubles: echoes rather than a depicted chapter (checkpoints and
  barracks that persist into a later era, a family's silence about a more recent loss) until the
  user decides how far the game goes there.

## 12. Respect and review

**Template:** whose voice the arc needs, what must be checked, and what the game may ship.

**Irish:** **(my call, proposed)**

- **Review by native speakers and a historian:** before anything ships, a native speaker
  reviews the language and a historian reviews the history. No Irish-language organization has
  published guidance on portraying the Hunger that the research could find. Use both names,
  An Gorta Mór and "the Famine", and avoid spectacle.
- **Open material the game can use, with attribution:**
  - the state's Irish place-name database (CC BY 4.0, through its developer API);
  - Mozilla Common Voice Irish recordings (CC0; mixed dialects, mostly Munster);
  - public-domain texts such as Douglas Hyde's 1892 lecture and older folklore collections;
  - the Noto Sans Ogham font (SIL OFL).
- **Material that needs written permission before anything is bundled:**
  - the leading Irish speech-synthesis service;
  - the standard online dictionaries (the game links out to these instead);
  - the 1930s schools' folklore collection.
- **Fonts and speech recognition:**
  - No open pixel font exists for the Gaelic script or ogham, so the game draws its own and
    ships it under OFL.
  - Speech recognition for Irish is too weak to be a core mechanic.
- **Handle with care:** some real events, for example the 1895 killing of Bridget Cleary over a
  changeling accusation, are real people's deaths.
